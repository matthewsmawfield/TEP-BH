## Abstract

The Temporal Equivalence Principle (TEP) proposes that virtual force carriers and statistical wavefunctions are tangent-limit descriptions of a deeper disformal geometry governed by the coupling B(φ). This paper derives an identifiability obstruction for mesoscopic tests of disformal kinematics and then executes a candidate decisive analysis programme on the Zimmermann et al. (2017) graphene Fabry-Perot dataset. On the theoretical side, we derive the disformal interferometric phase from the particle action on the disformal metric, showing that the TEP contribution is an additive phase perturbation δθ(x) = κ · T_{geom}(x), where T_{geom} is the line integral of B[φ(r)]|∇φ(r)|^{2} along the interferometer arm. The phase separates cleanly into dynamical, Aharonov-Bohm, and geometric terms, and the TEP correction is not reducible to any combination of carrier period, linear drift, or sinusoidal nuisance basis (verified by symbolic derivation and numerical rank tests with variance inflation factors below 6).

We freeze a geometry-locked predictor T_{geom} from an analytical electrostatic model of the graphene split-gate device, construct a joint generative model y = M_{conv} + κ · R_{c} · T_{geom} + ε with AR(1) correlated likelihood, and fit it to the raw Zimmermann et al. (2017) Fabry-Perot dataset. Seven conventional baseline models are compared; the single-carrier model (P ≈ 134 px) is the most robust by blocked cross-validation. Five phase extraction methods are audited with injection-recovery tests; local sinusoidal regression is selected as the most consistent. The joint model is then tested against six detection criteria. Zero of six criteria are met. The V_{T}/V_{R} opposite-sign fraction is 44–50%, exactly at chance (binomial p = 1.0). The amplitude κ is unstable across line cuts (ranging from −10 to +10, frequently hitting boundary constraints), flips sign between covariance models, and does not improve held-out prediction. Injection recovery reveals severe pipeline bias (±5κ even at zero injected signal).

The result is a pipeline-limited non-detection of a candidate mesoscopic proxy, not a quantitative null. A forensic analysis (F1–F9) identifies three specific errors in the previous pipeline: (1) the V_{T}/V_{R} criterion tested opposite phase instead of opposite voltage — the Fabry–P&eacute;rot scattering matrix predicts common phase with opposite voltage residuals, confirmed by phase correlation 0.981 and 100% opposite-voltage consistency across nine cuts (vs 44% for the wrong criterion); (2) the predictor uses |∇φ|² (isotropic) instead of (u·∇φ)² = |∇φ|² cos²(α) (directional), overestimating the signal by 15.5× because edge states propagate perpendicular to the field gradient for most of their path; (3) the predictor uses gate-voltage derivatives instead of real-space fields from a Poisson solver, overestimating by up to 2500×. The estimator bias is repaired from 8.55 to 0.365 by linearizing the model (κ outside the cosine). The measured κ = −0.32 is below the 95% detectability threshold (0.506, Fisher information I = 10.55). Nuisance models absorb 87–120% of injected signals. TEP does not presently provide a normalized device-scale screening law, so the question of whether this device should have detected TEP has no calculable answer. The non-detection constrains the tested proxy and analysis architecture, but not the underlying disformal coupling. Graphene quantum Hall interferometry remains a plausible candidate platform for mesoscopic TEP tests, but the Zimmermann dataset was not demonstrably a should-detect experiment.

On the theoretical side, the paper develops the interaction-kinematics layer: a U(1) phase-bundle construction from temporal vortex defects that satisfies the homogeneous Maxwell/Bianchi identity, geometric kinematics that recovers Coulomb-like behavior as a weak-field consistency condition, entanglement as an unbroken macroscopic geometric contour, and the probability wavefunction as a proposed physical volumetric shear-wake. With identical wide period bounds, the standard γ = 1 model and the TEP γ-free model achieve the same χ^{2} = 104.56, demonstrating a perfect P–γ degeneracy in which the effective period P/γ ≈ 135.8 px is the only identifiable quantity. This degeneracy is structural, not a pipeline artefact.

### 2.3 Geometric Kinematics: Attraction and Repulsion as Navigation

Particles attract or repel because they are navigating localized, direction-dependent light-cone tilts. Consider two charges q_{1} and q_{2} separated by distance r in a background with spatially varying B(φ). The disformal metric introduces an effective refractive index for temporal propagation:

n_{eff}(r) = √(1 + B(φ(r)) |∇φ|^{2} / A^{2}(φ)). (5)

A charge of opposite sign to the source experiences a region where n_{eff} increases toward the source, bending its trajectory inward: this is the geometric origin of Coulomb attraction. The trajectory equation follows from the geodesic equation in the disformal metric:

d^{2}x^{μ} / dτ^{2} + Γ^{μ}_{αβ}(g) (dx^{α}/dτ)(dx^{β}/dτ) = − C^{μ}_{αβ} (dx^{α}/dτ)(dx^{β}/dτ), (6)

where Γ^{μ}_{αβ}(g) is the Levi-Civita connection of the background metric g_{μν}, and C^{μ}_{αβ} = Γ̃^{μ}_{αβ} − Γ^{μ}_{αβ}(g) is the disformal correction: the difference between the Levi-Civita connection of the full metric g̃_{μν} = A^{2}g_{μν} + B ∇_{μ}φ ∇_{ν}φ and that of the background. This avoids double-counting the disformal connection. The right-hand side is a geometric force arising from the gradient of B(φ). For two like charges the gradient is repulsive (particles are deflected away from regions of higher B), while for opposite charges the effective sign of the coupling inverts and the gradient becomes attractive. No virtual photons are introduced as fundamental carriers in this geometric description; the standard virtual-photon expansion is recovered as the screened tangent-limit description.

The radial acceleration between two point charges in the weak-field limit (B ≪ 1) is

a_{r} = −λ_{B} (q_{1}q_{2} / m_{test}) ∇&Mscr;_{B}(φ) · r̂, (7)

where λ_{B} is a coupling constant with dimensions [kg · m^{3} / (C^{2} s^{2})] and &Mscr;_{B} = r_{0}/r is a dimensionless disformal potential with reference scale r_{0}. This reproduces Coulomb's law when the source equation or boundary condition fixes the dimensionless potential gradient so that ∇&Mscr;_{B} = ∇(r_{0}/r) = −r_{0} r̂ / r^{2} for a static charge distribution, with λ_{B} = 1/4πε_{0} absorbing the electromagnetic constants. **Proposition 3 (Coulomb recovery).** This is a conditional weak-field theorem: the geometric ansatz recovers the Coulomb form once the charge defect supplies the correct radial B(φ) gradient. It is not a derivation of Coulomb's law from first principles, but a consistency check that the disformal metric can reproduce the observed inverse-square law in the weak-field limit. Substituting: a_{r} = −λ_{B}(q_{1}q_{2}/m_{test})(−r_{0}r̂/r^{2}) = +λ_{B}(q_{1}q_{2}/m_{test})(r_{0}/r^{2})r̂. For like charges (q_{1}q_{2} > 0) the acceleration is positive in the r̂ direction, corresponding to repulsion; for opposite charges the sign inverts, giving attraction. No virtual photons are introduced as fundamental carriers in this geometric description.

### 2.4 Entanglement Geometry

Entanglement is defined mathematically as an unbroken macroscopic geometric contour connecting bifurcated topological charges. When a particle pair is created, the temporal field at the creation event carries a single connected phase contour. As the particles separate, each carries with it a phase singularity; the contour between them remains unbroken because the disformal metric retains memory of the shared origin.

Let the two-particle state be represented by two phase fields φ_{1}(x) and φ_{2}(x) centred at positions x_{1} and x_{2}. The entanglement contour is the geodesic γ(s) in the temporal shear field that connects the two charge cores. Its length in proper time is

L_{ent} = ∫_{γ} dτ = ∫_{γ} √(B(φ) |dφ/ds|^{2}) ds. (8)

The contour is unbroken when L_{ent} is finite and the integrand never vanishes along the path. A measurement on particle 1 samples the phase φ at x_{1}, which perturbs the entire contour because B(φ) couples the local phase to the metric. The perturbation propagates along the contour at the speed of temporal shear (the local light-cone tilt), not instantaneously, but because the contour is pre-existing and connected the correlation appears non-local in any isochronous slicing. There is no collapse of a wavefunction; there is only the geometric sampling of a shared, continuous temporal deformation.

The Schmidt rank of a bipartite entangled state is conjectured to map to the winding number of the phase contour around the charge pair. A maximally entangled Bell state corresponds to a contour with winding number ±1, while partially mixed states correspond to contours with multiple windings or decohered segments where the integrand intermittently vanishes. This mapping is an explicit conjecture: ordinary Schmidt decomposition applies to pure bipartite states, and the extension to mixed states via winding number has not been formally derived.

### 2.5 Bell Correlation from Contour Holonomy

The entanglement contour picture must reproduce the quantum correlation function if it is to be more than a geometric analogy. Consider a spin-1/2 singlet state prepared at the origin. For a maximally entangled state, the two transported spinors are anti-aligned when the measurement axes are parallel. C(*a*, *b*) = −cos θ_{ab}, (9)

where θ_{ab} is the holonomy angle of the orientation transport. This equals the quantum mechanical correlation because the SU(2) holonomy of the spin-1/2 representation gives exactly the rotation matrix R_{ij}(θ) = δ_{ij} cos θ + ε_{ijk} n̂_{k} sin θ + (1 − cos θ) n̂_{i} n̂_{j}. **Proposition 4 (Bell correlation reconstruction).** With E(*a*,*b*) = −cos(*a* − *b*), the CHSH parameter S = |E(*a*,*b*) − E(*a*,*b*′) + E(*a*′,*b*) + E(*a*′,*b*′)| achieves its quantum maximum S_{max} = 2√2 for the measurement directions *a* = 0, *a*′ = π/2, *b* = π/4, *b*′ = 3π/4. Note that the SU(2) group invoked here is the holonomy group of the temporal orientation bundle that governs spin-1/2, distinct from the SU(2) gauge group of the weak interaction discussed in Section 2.1. This is a conditional reconstruction: it assumes a spinorial SU(2) structure, singlet-like anti-alignment, and standard projection axes, then recovers −cos θ. It should be read as a quantitative consistency construction for the TEP contour ontology, not as a derivation of the full Hilbert-space structure or the general Schmidt-rank hierarchy from scalar-disformal geometry alone. The deterministic contour model does not, by itself, evade Bell's locality assumptions: a finite-speed pre-existing contour is compatible with Bell's theorem as a non-local hidden-variable model, and the relationship between the contour ontology and Bell's locality conditions requires further analysis.

### 2.6 Temporal Anisotropy and the Aharonov-Bohm Phase

In a disformal background the proper time elapsed along a trajectory depends on the local temporal shear. Consider an edge state circulating around a confined cavity of perimeter L. Inside the cavity the disformal coupling modifies the effective metric, so the proper time becomes

τ_{edge} = γ τ_{bulk}, (10)

where γ is a dimensionless temporal-anisotropy parameter. In principle γ is determined by the spatial variation of B(φ) and the cavity geometry, but its microscopic dependence is too complex to compute ab initio for the present device; in the empirical analysis it is therefore treated as a phenomenological fitting parameter (Section 5.3). Because phase accumulation is proportional to proper time, the effective phase inside the cavity is rescaled:

φ_{AB}^{edge} = γ φ_{AB}^{bulk}. (11)

When γ ≠ 1 the interference pattern is compressed or expanded relative to the bulk reference. A value γ < 1 corresponds to slower proper-time evolution inside the cavity — the edge-state clock runs slower than the bulk clock, the signature expected from disformal coupling in a confined geometry. **Proposition 2 (Aharonov-Bohm holonomy).** The Aharonov-Bohm phase φ_{AB} = 2πΦ/Φ_{0} arises from the non-trivial holonomy of the U(1) phase bundle around the defect core: the gauge field A_{μ} has non-trivial circulation ∮ ∇χ · d*l* = 2πn. This identification is a bundle matching condition, not an independent derivation of electromagnetic flux quantization. The holonomy is topological — it depends only on the winding number, not on the path geometry — and is therefore robust against local perturbations of the disformal metric.

## 3. Historical Reinterpretation: The Shear-Wake

### 3.1 The Probability Amplitude as Physical Shear-Wake

The Copenhagen Interpretation posits that the wavefunction ψ(x, t) is a probability amplitude whose squared modulus gives the likelihood of finding a particle at position x. TEP proposes an ontic reinterpretation: ψ is proposed to represent the physical volumetric shear-wake generated by a moving topological charge in the temporal field, rather than a distribution over possible outcomes.

When a particle moves through the disformal background, its topological charge core drags the surrounding temporal medium, creating a wake of phase shear that extends macroscopically. The wake is not a statistical guess about where the particle might be found; it is the real geometric deformation that the particle itself produced and continues to ride. The modulus |ψ| measures the amplitude of this shear, and the phase arg(ψ) measures the local tilt of the temporal surface. Measurement is the geometric interaction of a detector with this pre-existing shear field, not a discontinuous collapse of a probability distribution. The specific geometric constraints required for this volumetric shear-wake to propagate as a stable spinor map directly to the Dirac operator, as derived in the companion quantum foundations paper (TEP-QF, Paper 23).

### 3.2 The Double-Slit Experiment

In the double-slit arrangement a beam of particles is directed at a barrier containing two apertures. The Copenhagen account is that each particle passes through both slits simultaneously as a delocalized wave, interferes with itself, and collapses to a point upon detection. This account is unnecessary.

In the TEP framework the physical process is as follows. The topological charge core of the particle is a compact, topologically protected singularity in the temporal field. As it approaches the barrier, the core can pass through only one slit because it is a localized object. However, the macroscopic shear-wake — the volumetric phase churn that the core has been generating since its source — is extended. The wake is not confined to the core's immediate neighbourhood; it spans the transverse dimension of the beam. This wake washes through both slits.

On the far side of the barrier the two diffracted shear-wakes interfere. Regions of constructive interference correspond to paths of least temporal resistance: the local temporal shear is such that the particle's proper-time evolution is minimized along those trajectories. The core, which is a physical topological charge responding to the local gradient of the temporal field, is steered toward these low-resistance channels. It does not choose a path probabilistically; it surfs the geometric interference pattern that its own wake created.

The "wave-particle duality" is therefore represented as a geometric process rather than a paradox. The particle is always a particle (a topological charge core), and the wave is always a wave (a physical shear-wake). They are two aspects of the same topological object moving through a dynamical temporal background. Under this interpretation, the observed interference pattern is not evidence that the particle went through both slits; it is evidence that the particle's wake went through both slits, and the core subsequently followed the wake's interference geometry.

### 3.3 Quantum Tunneling

Tunneling is conventionally described as a particle probabilistically leaking through a classically forbidden barrier. The WKB transmission coefficient T ≈ exp(−2∫ dx √(2m(V − E)) / ℏ) is interpreted as the probability that the particle is found on the far side. TEP represents this as a geometric process instead.

In the TEP framework the barrier is a region of spacetime where the disformal coupling B(φ) is elevated, creating a steep temporal shear gradient. The particle's core cannot propagate through this region by ordinary geodesic motion because the effective refractive index n_{eff} becomes too large. However, the particle's shear-wake is not so constrained. The wake is a non-local deformation of the temporal field; it penetrates the barrier because the temporal medium itself is continuous and the shear disturbance propagates according to the wave equation for φ in the disformal metric:

□_{g} φ + ∂_{μ}(B(φ) ∂^{μ}φ) = 0. (12)

Inside the barrier the wake establishes a temporary, highly localized disformal geometric bridge: a narrow channel where B(φ) is depressed by the incoming shear, creating a transient low-resistance path. The core, responding to the local gradient, is drawn through this channel. The process is not probabilistic leakage; it is the geometric navigation of a topological charge through a shear-induced deformation of the temporal landscape. The apparent exponential suppression arises because the wake amplitude decays with the spatial extent of the high-B region, reproducing WKB-like attenuation under the stated identification of B(φ) with the effective potential barrier.

## 5. Empirical Test: Aharonov-Bohm 2+1D

### 5.1 Dataset and Experimental Geometry

The published Fabry-Perot quantum Hall interferometry dataset of Zimmermann et al. (Nat. Commun. 8, 14983, 2017), publicly archived on Zenodo (record 4430703), is re-analysed. The data comprise a 151 × 151 gate-sweep map of a monolayer graphene device at high magnetic field, acquired at base temperature in a dilution refrigerator. Two lock-in channels are recorded: transmission (VT) and reflection (VR), both scaled to units of kΩ.

The axes are: V_{sg} = [−4.0, +4.0] V (split-gate voltage) and V_{bg} = [−0.96, +2.5] V (back-gate voltage). A line cut is extracted between the pixel coordinates A = (66, 3) and B = (21, 115), reproducing the cut used in the original MATLAB analysis script supplied by the authors. The raw profiles are smoothed with a moving-average window of 15 points to suppress high-frequency noise.

### 5.2 Oscillation Period and FFT Characterisation

Fast Fourier transform of the smoothed transmission profile reveals a strong spectral component at 94.52 pixels with power 72325.48 (reflection channel: 94.52 pixels, 59791.90). This is the dominant oscillation scale of the Fabry-Perot pattern. The Fabry-Perot intensity T(φ) ∝ [1 + F sin^{2}(φ/2)]^{−1}, where F = 4R/(1 − R)^{2} is the coefficient of finesse and R the cavity reflectivity, is not a pure sinusoid and its Fourier spectrum contains even harmonics; the fitted period may therefore differ from the FFT-derived value. ### 5.3 TEP Interference Model

The phenomenological model used to fit the Fabry-Perot interference pattern is a cosine with polynomial background drift:

I(x) = A cos(2πx / P + φ_{0}) + Bx + Cx^{2} + D, (13)

where P is the oscillation period and the linear and quadratic terms account for slow background drift. In the TEP framework, the proper-time elapsed by a particle traversing the edge channel depends on the local temporal shear. The effective phase accumulation is therefore rescaled by a temporal-anisotropy parameter γ:

I_{TEP}(x) = A cos(γ · 2πx / P + φ_{0}) + Bx + Cx^{2} + D. (14)

When γ = 1 the TEP model reduces to the standard expression; when γ ≠ 1 the interference pattern is compressed or expanded. The parameter γ is a phenomenological proxy for the integrated conformal and disformal effect along the edge channel; the underlying Temporal Equivalence Principle defines clock-rate rescaling through the conformal factor A(φ) and null-cone tilts through B(φ). The Fabry-Perot interferometer is, at its core, a stopwatch: it measures the proper-time interval for a photon to traverse the cavity and return. A temporal shear wave altering the local ticking rate therefore manifests as an apparent spatial phase shift in the interference pattern, even though the spatial geometry of the cavity itself is unchanged. Crucially, γ appears in the model only as the ratio γ/P, multiplying the carrier wavenumber. This means that a global γ rescaling is mathematically degenerate with an effective period P_{eff} = P/γ: any fit achieved by γ ≠ 1 can be reproduced by γ = 1 with a correspondingly adjusted P. The global γ parameterisation therefore cannot, in principle, distinguish a TEP signal from a period shift on a single 1D line cut.

### 5.4 Model Comparison: The P–γ Degeneracy

Both models are fitted to the smoothed transmission profile by nonlinear least squares (L-BFGS-B, max 5000 iterations, 30 random restarts, seed = 42). The standard model fixes γ = 1 (six parameters); the TEP model allows γ to vary in the range 0.3 ≤ γ ≤ 2.0 (seven parameters). Both models share identical period bounds (20 ≤ P ≤ 500 px), ensuring a fair comparison.

The result is a perfect degeneracy: both models converge to identical χ^{2} = 104.56, with γ = 1.000 and P = 135.9 px. The TEP model's seventh parameter (γ) is not identified by the data; the optimizer drives it to unity and compensates entirely through P. The effective period P/γ = 135.9 px is the only identifiable quantity. A nominal regular-model BIC calculation penalises the redundant γ coordinate by ΔBIC = BIC_{TEP} − BIC_{std} = 5.23, but conventional BIC is not formally applicable to this singular parameterisation: the Fisher information is singular because γ enters only as γ/P, making the parameter redundant and the model non-regular. The correct conclusion is that the two parameterisations are observationally equivalent over the relevant effective-period domain; the parameter γ cannot be inferred independently.

This degeneracy is not a pipeline deficiency or a bound-tuning artefact. It is a structural property of the model: because γ enters only as γ/P, the likelihood surface contains a flat valley along the ridge P/γ = constant. A profile likelihood scan over γ (fixing γ at 200 values from 0.3 to 2.0 and re-optimising all other parameters) confirms that χ^{2} is flat to within 10^{−6} across the entire range. The degeneracy can only be broken by a model in which γ affects something other than the global effective period — for example, a position-dependent γ(x) that modulates the phase locally rather than globally.

The disformal coupling B(φ)(∂φ)^{2} is not a uniform rescaling. It peaks where |∂φ|^{2} is maximal — at the split-gate constriction centre, where the electric field gradient is strongest — and vanishes in the bulk. The TEP signal is therefore a localised phase perturbation, not a global period shift. The topography regression of Section 5.5 analyses the residual phase shift after standard-model subtraction. However, the topography regression does not entirely bypass the P–γ degeneracy: a global γ rescaling produces a linear phase ramp Δθ(x) = (γ − 1) · 2πx/P, which is exactly the linear model in the topography table. The linear topography result is therefore another expression of the original degeneracy, not an independent test. Only a nonlinear, independently frozen predictor orthogonal to the carrier and drift basis — such as a geometry-derived B(φ)|∇φ|^{2} field — would genuinely bypass the obstruction.

### 5.5 Disformal Topography of the Cavity

Before extracting the phase perturbation, the best conventional carrier baseline must be established. A two-frequency model — two cosines with independent periods, amplitudes, and phases, plus the same polynomial background — was fitted alongside the single-frequency model. The two-frequency model is strongly preferred (ΔBIC = -142.07, χ^{2} = 44.77 vs 104.56), with periods P_{1} = 46.03 px (amplitude A_{1} = 0.855) and P_{2} = 145.93 px (amplitude A_{2} = 3.013). This confirms the carrier period audit finding below: the single-frequency model at P = 135.86 px leaves substantial residual structure. The second component could represent a harmonic, nonstationary frequency, edge-state mixing, or a flexible approximation to a distorted Fabry-Perot waveform; the two-frequency model is a phenomenological fit, not a proof of two physically distinct device modes. **Caveat:** this ΔBIC is computed on the moving-average-smoothed line cut using an ordinary independent-error BIC, which may substantially overstate the evidence when all 186 smoothed samples are treated as independent. Smoothing creates correlated observations; the correlation corrections applied to the phase-topography analysis have not been applied to this model comparison. The quoted ΔBIC should therefore be treated as a descriptive fit comparison, not as discovery-grade evidence for two conventional components. Additionally, the long-period component P_{2} = 145.93 px spans only approximately 1.27 cycles over the 186-sample window; a sinusoid spanning barely more than one cycle can be strongly confounded with polynomial background drift, amplitude modulation, a chirp, or edge effects. No profile-likelihood intervals or bootstrap confidence intervals are reported for P_{1}, P_{2}, their amplitudes or phases. Until correlated-likelihood and out-of-sample validation are performed, 'two resolved spectral components' remains stronger than the data support. All downstream phase extraction uses the dominant carrier (P_{2} = 145.93 px, A_{2} = 3.013) from the two-frequency baseline.

The vector-potential interpretation of the Aharonov-Bohm phase is not required by the data. If the phase shift Δθ(x) = θ(x) − θ_{std}(x) is instead regressed directly against the disformal metric tilt B(φ) itself, the cavity's residual phase structure can be characterised. Phase extraction was performed by complex demodulation with a Gaussian low-pass filter (cutoff at P/4), which preserves higher-frequency phase perturbations that a Savitzky-Golay window of round(P) would over-smooth. Five physically motivated shapes for B(φ) are tested: uniform (constant offset, corresponding to a phase-origin or calibration nuisance), linear (monotonic gradient across the cavity, corresponding to the global γ/P degeneracy in residual form), Gaussian (confinement peak at the split-gate centre), harmonic (periodic modulation from cavity geometry), and exponential (Laplace-like boundary decay). Each is fitted to the measured phase shift by ordinary least squares, with the regression amplitude κ and intercept absorbing all calibration and unit conversions.

The model comparison proceeds in three stages, each addressing a distinct statistical concern.

**Step 1 — Naive BIC (the baseline).** On the uncorrected BIC, the linear model ranks first (BIC = -404.02, χ^{2} = 20.03), and the uniform model ranks second (BIC = -403.09; ΔBIC = 0.9). The harmonic (-394.16; ΔBIC = 9.9), exponential (-389.22; ΔBIC = 14.8), and Gaussian (-389.08; ΔBIC = 14.9) profiles all rank below uniform. Only the linear model outperforms the uniform model; the boundary-dominated profiles (exponential, Gaussian) do not. Because severe autocorrelation invalidates ordinary BIC discrimination, none of these rankings should be treated as a resolved physical preference.

**Step 2 — Correlation-corrected BIC_{eff} (the correction).** The complex demodulation uses a Gaussian low-pass with an effective window of 36 points (approximately P/4), which introduces strong autocorrelation between adjacent phase samples. The autocorrelation-based effective sample size (Bayley–Hammersley estimator) collapses to n_{eff} = 5, and the correlation-corrected BIC_{eff} compresses all models into a narrow range (8.71 to 13.50). On BIC_{eff}, the uniform model has the lowest value (8.71), with the linear model close behind (10.16). This compression makes point-estimate rankings unreliable: BIC is an asymptotic approximation and is not trustworthy when n_{eff} = 5 and the number of fitted parameters is comparable to the effective sample size. No BIC_{eff} ranking is meaningful at n_{eff} = 5.

**Step 3 — Bootstrap resampling (limitations).** A pointwise bootstrap over the filtered phase samples (n = 1,000) was performed. However, this bootstrap is not statistically valid when n_{eff} = 5: resampling individual samples destroys the original correlation structure, treats correlated pseudoreplicates as independent observations, and produces confidence intervals that are artificially narrow. With n ≈ 186 and a complex-demodulation effective window near 36 samples, there are approximately five independent blocks. A conventional pointwise bootstrap cannot recover discovery-grade information from this line cut.

A parametric bootstrap that fits an AR(1) model to the phase residuals and generates correlated realisations was implemented as a sensitivity analysis. This AR(1) bootstrap is not a statistically definitive replacement for the pointwise bootstrap: the fitted coefficient φ_{AR} = 0.9961 is near-unit-root, and the n_{eff} diagnostic table (Table 5a) shows that different estimators yield values ranging from 0.36 to 5, indicating that a single AR(1) coefficient does not fully characterise the covariance structure. The bootstrap should be read as an AR(1)-specific sensitivity check, not as the definitive correlated analysis. A complete treatment would include local-level/random-walk state-space models, Mat&eacute;rn Gaussian-process covariance, ARMA models, and spectral phase-randomised noise, with joint mean-covariance estimation. A near-unit-root covariance model can absorb a genuine smooth physical signal as noise, so this is a credible false-negative mechanism (Section 5.8, mechanism 6). Across 500 correlated realisations, no tested model pair is decisively distinguished. The ΔBIC for linear vs uniform has mean 95.67, median 68.22, 95% CI [-5.06, 329.74], 68% CI [3.85, 193.04], with 0.112 of realisations yielding ΔBIC < 0. The large mean relative to the median indicates a right-skewed distribution driven by occasional extreme realisations, not a stable central tendency. Similarly, the CIs for linear vs Gaussian (mean ΔBIC = 31.96, CI [-15.60, 189.45]), linear vs harmonic, and linear vs exponential all include zero. Here ΔBIC = BIC_{model} − BIC_{linear}; a positive value means the model is worse than linear. Under the AR(1) correlation model, no single B(φ) shape can be distinguished from the others. The parametric bootstrap confirms that the point-estimate BIC_{eff} rankings are not stable under correlated resampling.

The r.m.s. phase-shift fluctuation is 0.3337 rad, consistent with the weak-tilt regime where Δθ < 1 rad throughout the cavity. After accounting approximately for correlation, the single line cut contains too little independent information to distinguish linear, uniform, exponential, harmonic, or Gaussian residual profiles. The residual does not exclude a small localised perturbation, but it provides no affirmative evidence for one. This does not contradict the TEP framework; it reflects the fundamental information limit of a single 1D cut through a heavily smoothed 2D map.

The variance-inflation factor of the TEP predictor is VIF = ∞, with condition number 479.45 and design rank 4 out of 5 columns. The TEP predictor T_{TEP}(x) = x_{norm} is collinear with the linear drift basis, making it structurally non-identifiable from conventional linear phase drift on this line cut. This is an identifiability result, not an empirical rejection: the linear TEP profile cannot be distinguished from a conventional linear drift, regardless of data quality. The critical comparison is Model C versus Model D: since T_{TEP} = x_{norm} is collinear with x, Model C and Model D have identical column spaces. A critical caveat applies to this identifiability result. The variable x is an index along a diagonal trajectory through (V_{sg}, V_{bg}) gate-voltage space; it is not a spatial coordinate across the graphene cavity. The predictor T_{TEP}(x) = x_{norm} therefore represents a linear combination of two experimental control voltages, not a physical temporal-field gradient. A real disformal response should be linked to quantities such as the electric-field magnitude near the split gates, the carrier-density gradient, the constriction width, the edge-state separation, the cavity area, the boundary curvature, the local filling factor, or the electrostatic potential gradient. Those quantities are generally nonlinear functions of (V_{sg}, V_{bg}) and need not resemble a linear function of the line-cut index. The infinite VIF result shows only that a predictor defined as x_{norm} cannot be separated from linear drift; it does not show that a physically calculated B(φ)|∇φ|^{2} predictor would be non-identifiable. The identifiability obstruction established here applies to the generic line-cut predictor, not to a geometry-derived disformal predictor.

A phase extraction method comparison was performed to audit the filter bandwidth. The original Savitzky-Golay window of round(P) ≈ 137 samples on 186 data points over-smooths, potentially suppressing real phase perturbations. Based on this comparison, the principal topography results in this paper use complex demodulation with Gaussian low-pass (cutoff P/4) as the phase extraction method. A localized TEP signature narrower than roughly one carrier period may still be partially suppressed even by this shorter filter; sensitivity to localized features remains uncalibrated under free-carrier and multi-frequency conditions and is a required component of the recovery analysis (Section 5.9).

The discrepancy between the Hessian and bootstrap correlations arises because the Hessian measures the local curvature of a flat valley, while the bootstrap samples the global landscape including non-linear regions where the degeneracy is partially broken by bounds.

The conclusion from the control suite is definitive: the global γ parameterisation is degenerate with P_{eff} = P/γ and cannot identify a TEP signal on a single 1D line cut. This is not a weakness of the TEP framework but a feature of the experimental geometry. The disformal coupling B(φ)(∂φ)^{2} produces a spatially varying phase perturbation, not a uniform period rescaling. The topography regression of Section 5.5 analyses this spatially varying perturbation directly. However, it does not entirely bypass the P–γ degeneracy: the linear model in the topography table corresponds to the global γ/P degeneracy in residual form. Only a nonlinear, independently frozen predictor orthogonal to the carrier and drift basis would genuinely bypass the obstruction. The topography regression's discriminating power is further limited by n_{eff} = 5 on this single line cut.

### 5.7 Spatial Null Tests and Injection Recovery (Exploratory)

As an exploratory spatial check, permutation null tests were performed on the full 2D map. The 2D residual field is the raw amplitude residual from step 05: a 2D standard Fabry-Perot model (cosine carrier with Gaussian envelope, second harmonic, and quadratic background) is fitted to the full V_{T}(V_{sg}, V_{bg}) map, and the residual R = V_{T} − M_{std,2D} is saved. This is an amplitude residual, not a phase residual. The TEP predictor is the 2D field T(V_{sg}, V_{bg}) = B(φ)|∇φ|^{2} from step 06, where φ is a phenomenological phase proxy φ ∝ V_{sg} · V_{bg} and B(φ) is a Gaussian confinement weight. The predictor is normalised and regressed against the residual by ordinary least squares. The observed ΔBIC between the TEP predictor and the null predictor is -33.17, with a regression coefficient α = 0.1383. Across 200 shuffle realisations, the mean null ΔBIC is -135.79 with empirical p-value = 1.0000.

This 2D amplitude-residual test is distinct from the full-map phase-field analysis proposed in Section 5.9. The present test uses a raw amplitude residual and a phenomenological predictor; the proposed analysis would extract a 2D phase field, use a geometry-derived predictor, and fit conventional and TEP terms jointly. The present test is therefore a coarse spatial check on whether the phenomenological predictor has explanatory power beyond chance, not a full 2D disformal phase analysis.

However, the null distribution requires careful interpretation. The ΔBIC is defined as BIC_{with predictor} − BIC_{null (α=0)}; a more negative value indicates that the predictor improves the fit. The observed ΔBIC = -33.17, while the mean null ΔBIC = -135.79. The null realisations achieve more negative ΔBIC values than the observation, meaning the shuffled predictors improve the fit more than the real predictor. The empirical p-value, computed with the correct tail direction (ΔBIC ≤ observed, since more negative is better), is p = 1.0000. The spatial-coherence claim is therefore not supported: the real predictor does not outperform shuffled predictors.

In addition to the axis-shuffle nulls, spectrum-preserving surrogates were constructed to test whether the TEP predictor outperforms fields with matched spatial smoothness and bandwidth. Four surrogate types were added: toroidal shift (circular wrap in both axes), axis reflection (flip along V_{sg} or V_{bg}), 180° rotation, and 2D phase randomisation (Fourier-space phase scrambling with preserved power spectrum). These surrogates preserve the spatial correlation structure that row/column shuffling destroys, providing a more stringent null. The individual null p-values are: toroidal shift p = 0.7960, 2D phase randomisation p = 0.8259. The individual tests provide no evidence that the phenomenological predictor outperforms matched-spectrum surrogates. A Fisher's combined p-value of p = 0.3579 was computed but should not be treated as a formal test: the individual null tests share the same data and predictor, so their p-values are correlated, violating Fisher's independence assumption. The combined value is reported as a descriptive summary only. All null values and the observed statistic are published in `results/08_null_tests.json` for independent verification.

Synthetic injection recovery tests calibrate the pipeline's detection sensitivity. Synthetic Fabry-Perot data were generated with known γ ≠ 1 signals injected at varying amplitudes. Crucially, the injection test fixes the carrier period to the known synthetic value rather than allowing it to vary freely. This breaks the P–γ degeneracy: when P is fixed, a global γ rescaling cannot be absorbed into a period shift, and the TEP model's extra parameter becomes identifiable. The pipeline achieves 100% detection power for γ ≥ 1.01 under this fixed-period protocol. This establishes that the pipeline is not intrinsically blind to disformal phase perturbations when the period is known. However, the real-data analysis allows P to vary freely, so the injection test does not replicate the same degeneracy conditions.

An equivalence test was implemented to confirm the degeneracy directly. Rather than running two separately optimised nonlinear models (which introduces an optimiser artefact when the standard model finds a slightly inferior local minimum), the test uses direct P_{eff} construction. A global γ = 1.10 signal is injected into synthetic data, and the standard model (γ = 1, free P) is fitted once with 30 random restarts. For each of six γ test values, the equivalent TEP parameters are constructed analytically: P_{TEP} = γ · P_{std}, keeping all other parameters identical. The TEP prediction A cos(γ · 2πx/P_{TEP} + φ) + Bx + Cx^{2} + off is then compared to the standard prediction A cos(2πx/P_{std} + φ) + Bx + Cx^{2} + off. Since γ/P_{TEP} = 1/P_{std}, the predictions are identical by construction. The sign convention is Δχ^{2} = χ^{2}_{TEP} − χ^{2}_{std} and ΔBIC = BIC_{TEP} − BIC_{std}; the expected values are Δχ^{2} = 0 and ΔBIC = ln(n) = 5.99 (one-parameter parsimony penalty). GATE 1 asserts that the nested TEP model must not fit worse (χ^{2}_{TEP} ≤ χ^{2}_{std} + ε); it passes with zero violations. GATE 1b asserts exact equivalence: max |pred_{TEP} − pred_{std}| < 10^{−10}, max |Δχ^{2}| < 10^{−8}, and max |ΔBIC − ln(n)| < 10^{−6}. The maximum prediction difference is 0.00, the maximum |Δχ^{2}| is 0.00, and the maximum |ΔBIC − ln(n)| is 0.00. When period is free, the γ parameter is absorbed into P_{eff} = P/γ and the extra parameter adds only the ln(n) parsimony penalty.

Four hard gates are implemented to ensure statistical validity. GATE 1 (nested χ^{2} assertion): PASS — the TEP model never fits worse than the standard model in synthetic equivalence tests. GATE 1b (exact equivalence assertion): PASS — requires max |pred_{TEP} − pred_{std}| < 10^{−10}, max |Δχ^{2}| < 10^{−8}, and max |ΔBIC − ln(n)| < 10^{−6} across all tested γ values. The gate passes by direct construction: since γ/P_{TEP} = 1/P_{std}, the predictions are identical to machine precision. (Gates 2 and 3 are reserved for future spatial-coherence and cross-device replication checks and are not activated in the current single-device analysis.) GATE 4 (design matrix rank): PASS — all generic B(φ) regression design matrices (linear, uniform, harmonic, exponential, Gaussian) are full rank after intercept removal for constant predictors. This gate applies to the topography design matrices, not to Model C of the identifiability test, which is intentionally rank deficient by construction. GATE 5 (BIC identity): PASS — BIC values match the formula n·log(χ^{2}/n) + k·log(n). GATE 6 (deterministic rerun): PASS — fixed seed produces identical outputs across runs. All gates pass.

Cross-device replication is currently limited by data availability. Only one published dataset (Zimmermann et al., 2017) provides the full raw measurement file required for this analysis. A systematic search of Zenodo, arXiv, Figshare, and PubMed found no additional raw Fabry-Perot quantum Hall interferometry datasets with sufficient detail. A cross-device meta-analysis framework has been prepared for future data ingest, but at present no independent replication exists.

### 5.8 False-Negative Mechanisms

The non-detection results established above — the P–γ degeneracy, the non-identifiability of the linear predictor, the failure of spatial nulls, and the bootstrap indistinguishability — do not exclude disformal phase structure in the graphene device. They exclude the global-γ parameterisation and show that a generic linear line-cut predictor is non-identifiable. A credible false-negative route remains. We identify seven specific mechanisms by which the analysis could have missed a real TEP signal. These mechanisms also explain why the current non-detection is classified as pipeline-limited rather than quantitative.

**(1) The line-cut coordinate is not physical position.** The variable x is an index along a diagonal trajectory through (V_{sg}, V_{bg}) gate-voltage space, not a spatial coordinate across the graphene cavity. The predictor T_{TEP}(x) = x_{norm} represents a linear combination of two experimental control voltages, not a physical temporal-field gradient. A real disformal response should be linked to the electric-field magnitude near the split gates, the carrier-density gradient, the constriction width, the edge-state separation, the cavity area, the boundary curvature, the local filling factor, or the electrostatic potential gradient. Those quantities are generally nonlinear functions of (V_{sg}, V_{bg}) and need not resemble a linear function of the line-cut index. The infinite VIF result shows only that a predictor defined as x_{norm} cannot be separated from linear drift; it does not show that a physically calculated B(φ)|∇φ|^{2} predictor would be non-identifiable.

**(2) The wrong observable may have been tested.** The paper correctly proves that global γ is degenerate with carrier period (P_{eff} = P/γ). That means γ was never a viable discovery observable in this geometry. But a real TEP signal could appear as local phase curvature rather than global phase slope; as asymmetric distortion of interference fringes; as a relative phase difference between V_{T} and V_{R}; as arm-dependent phase in an unequal-path geometry; as a change in harmonic content; as localized fringe compression near the constriction; as deformation of the full 2D interference ridge; or as coupling between amplitude and phase near a boundary gradient. The failure of global γ does not imply failure of disformal kinematics; it may mean the pipeline tested a reparameterisation of the standard period.

**(3) The phase extractor may erase a localized signal.** Although the principal analysis now uses complex demodulation with a Gaussian low-pass (cutoff P/4, effective window ≈ 34), a localized TEP signature narrower than roughly one carrier period may still be partially suppressed. The original Savitzky-Golay window of round(P) ≈ 137 on 186 points would have over-smoothed almost completely. The injection recovery tests of Section 5.7 quantify the sensitivity under the fixed-period protocol, but a localized perturbation under free-period conditions has not been injected.

**(4) The two-frequency model may absorb a real local modulation.** The two-frequency model is strongly preferred (ΔBIC = -142.07), indicating that the single-carrier fit was inadequate. But a flexible two-frequency model can also approximate frequency modulation, phase warping, localized fringe compression, or a slowly varying disformal perturbation. It may absorb a genuine TEP effect and relabel it as a second conventional cosine. This does not mean the two-frequency result is wrong; it means the nuisance model may be too flexible to serve as a neutral baseline. Validation requires injecting a known localized TEP phase perturbation, fitting the two-frequency nuisance model, and measuring how much of the injected signal it absorbs.

**(5) Residual fitting may be the wrong analysis architecture.** The current pipeline fits a conventional model, subtracts it, extracts a phase residual, and then fits TEP shapes to that residual. This sequential procedure treats the conventional fit as known exactly and does not propagate uncertainty in carrier period, carrier amplitude, phase offset, second frequency, background drift, or covariance parameters. A TEP signal can be partially absorbed during the conventional fit and therefore disappear before the residual is analysed. The correct model is joint: y(x) = M_{conventional}(x; η) + κ T_{TEP}(x) + ε, with all conventional parameters η, the TEP amplitude κ, and the covariance model inferred simultaneously.

**(6) The AR(1) model may classify signal as noise.** The estimated AR coefficient φ_{AR} = 0.9961 is almost a unit-root process. This may reflect true filter-induced covariance, but it can also occur when a deterministic low-frequency structure remains in the residual. If that structure is absorbed into the noise covariance, a real smooth signal can be declared statistically insignificant. The mean function and covariance must be fitted jointly, with sensitivity checks against Mat&eacute;rn kernels, Gaussian-process covariance, ARMA models, Fourier-domain correlated noise, and state-space oscillator models.

**(7) One line cut may miss the relevant region.** The full dataset contains a 151 × 151 map, but the principal analysis uses one line cut selected to reproduce the original authors' MATLAB example. That cut was not chosen to maximise sensitivity to TEP. It may avoid the strongest confinement boundary, cross the field gradient obliquely, mix split-gate and back-gate effects, combine regions with opposite predicted signs, or average over a localized anomaly. A signal could be present elsewhere in the map while being diluted on this particular trajectory.

### 5.9 Candidate Decisive Analysis: Geometry-Locked Predictor and Joint Generative Model

The false-negative mechanisms identified in Section 5.8 each correspond to a specific analysis. The six-step programme outlined in the previous version of this paper has now been executed. We report the results here, together with the fundamental limitations that prevent the outcome from being classified as a quantitative test.

**Step 1 — Geometry-derived TEP predictor.** We construct T_{geom}(V_{sg}, V_{bg}) from an analytical electrostatic model of the graphene split-gate device. The model computes the split-gate potential U_{sg} using error-function profiles for semi-infinite gates with a 200 nm gap through 30 nm hBN dielectric, plus a uniform back-gate field U_{bg}. The disformal invariant |∇U|^{2} = (∂U/∂V_{sg})^{2} + (∂U/∂V_{bg})^{2} is computed on the full 151 × 151 gate-voltage grid. A model hierarchy T0–T3 is frozen before unblinding: T0 = 0 (null), T1 = G(|∇U|^{2}) (gradient only), T2 = G(|∇U|^{2}) · W(d_{boundary}) (gradient × boundary proximity), T3 = G(|∇U|^{2}) · W(d_{boundary}) · C(ℓ_{coh}) (gradient × boundary × coherence). The predictor is hashed (SHA-256) and frozen before comparison with measured residuals. A numerical rank test confirms that all three predictors (T1, T2, T3) are non-collinear with the conventional nuisance basis [1, x, cos(2πx/P), sin(2πx/P)]: rank = 5 (full), VIF ≤ 5.4, orthogonal norm ratio ≥ 0.43. Gate 2 passes.

**Step 2 — Conventional baseline.** Seven conventional models are fitted to the raw (unsmoothed) V_{T} line cut with AR(1) correlated likelihood: M1 (single carrier + quadratic background), M2 (fundamental + constrained harmonic), M3 (two unconstrained carriers), M4 (chirped carrier), M5 (amplitude-modulated carrier), M6 (two-carrier with edge-state mixing), M7 (damped sinusoid). The single-carrier model M1 (P ≈ 134 px, φ_{AR} = 0.94) is the most robust by blocked cross-validation (CV MSE = 10.25). The two-carrier model M3 improves in-sample NLL (40.79 vs 50.36) but fails out-of-sample (CV MSE = 11.24), indicating overfitting. Gate 4 fails: the second carrier does not survive held-out prediction.

**Step 3 — Phase extractor audit.** Five extraction methods are compared: complex demodulation, local sinusoidal regression, Hilbert transform, Morlet wavelet ridge, and Kalman-filter phase tracking. Injection-recovery tests with Gaussian phase perturbations of varying width, amplitude, and second-carrier contamination show that local sinusoidal regression has the lowest combined bias and position error (Gate 5 selected method). Cross-method consistency is concerning: complex demodulation and local regression correlate at only 0.63, and complex demodulation vs Hilbert is negatively correlated (−0.65), indicating that the choice of extraction method significantly affects the results.

**Step 4 — Joint generative model.** We construct y(x) = A cos(kx + φ + κ · T_{geom}) + Bx + Cx^{2} + offset + ε, where ε follows AR(1) with parameter φ_{AR}, and fit simultaneously for all parameters including κ. The model is fitted to both V_{T} and V_{R} channels with all three predictors (T1, T2, T3). Gate 6 fails for all predictors: κ does not match the predicted positive sign for T1 and T2 (both negative), the V_{T}/V_{R} opposite-sign prediction is not confirmed, blocked cross-validation does not improve over the conventional-only model, and the AR(1) coefficient hits the boundary (φ_{AR} = 0.999) for T3, indicating overfitting rather than detection.

**Step 5 — Full-map V_{T}/V_{R} replication.** The joint model is fitted on nine line cuts across the full 151 × 151 map. The V_{T}/V_{R} opposite-sign fraction is 50% for T1, 44% for T2, and 44% for T3 — exactly at chance level (binomial p = 1.0 for all three). The fitted κ values are wildly unstable across line cuts, ranging from −10 to +10 with many values hitting the boundary constraints. The carrier period P is equally unstable (23–500 px across cuts). There is no spatial consistency in the TEP amplitude.

**Step 6 — Injection recovery, covariance stress, and held-out validation.** Injection recovery tests reveal severe pipeline bias: even with zero injected signal (κ_{inj} = 0), the recovered κ has a mean bias of −1.8 to −4.0 and standard deviation of 3.6–5.1, depending on the predictor. The pipeline cannot reliably distinguish a true signal from noise at these amplitudes. Covariance stress testing shows that κ flips sign between white-noise (+0.19), AR(1) (−0.19), and ARMA(1,1) (−0.18) covariance models for T1, and changes dramatically for T2 and T3 as well. Held-out validation (60% train, 40% test) produces κ values of 9.19, −7.91, and −0.23 for T1, T2, T3 respectively — none consistent with the full-data fits.

**Result: Pipeline-Limited Non-Detection.** Six detection criteria are specified before unblinding: (1) κ excludes zero, (2) sign matches the frozen prediction, (3) V_{T}/V_{R} opposite-sign replication, (4) blocked cross-validation improves, (5) κ stable across line cuts, (6) κ survives covariance stress. Zero of six criteria are met. However, this outcome is not a quantitative null. Three fundamental limitations prevent classifying the result as a quantitative test of disformal kinematics.

**(1) No predicted amplitude.** TEP does not presently provide a normalized device-scale screening law. The mesoscopic screening operator S_{Σ}^{meso} cannot predict whether the expected phase shift is 10^{−3}, 10^{−1}, or 10 radians. Without a predicted amplitude, the question of whether this device should have detected TEP has no calculable answer. A theory can only be challenged by non-detection when the predicted amplitude exceeds the demonstrated detection threshold with adequately calibrated power. Neither side of that inequality is presently secure.

**(2) Estimator not calibrated.** Zero-injection recovery produces bias of ±5κ, false-positive rates far from the declared significance level, and sign instability across covariance models. An estimator that returns nonzero amplitude when zero signal was injected cannot produce a quantitative bound. The non-detection may reflect pipeline pathology rather than the absence of a physical signal.

**(3) Predictor partly phenomenological.** The geometry-locked predictor uses gate-voltage derivatives as proxies for real-space electric-field gradients. The V_{T}/V_{R} opposite-sign prediction has not been derived from the device scattering matrix — a common phase perturbation δθ_{R} = δθ_{T} can produce opposite voltage residuals δV_{R} ≈ −δV_{T} through the transfer function, so the observed 44–50% opposite-sign fraction may be testing the wrong criterion. The predictor is an informed proxy, not yet a corpus-derived TEP field solution.

The correct classification is a pipeline-limited non-detection of a candidate mesoscopic proxy. The non-detection constrains the tested proxy and analysis architecture, but not the underlying disformal coupling. Graphene quantum Hall interferometry is a plausible candidate platform for mesoscopic TEP tests because it combines coherent phase accumulation with tunable boundary geometry. However, TEP does not presently provide a normalized device-scale screening law or a first-principles mapping from gate electrostatics to the temporal scalar field. The Zimmermann dataset therefore cannot yet be classified as an experiment in which a detectable TEP response was predicted. The major result of this paper is the identification of what must be calculated before the question becomes meaningful: (a) a normalized mesoscopic screening law with fixed amplitude prediction; (b) a real-space electrostatic model mapping device geometry to the TEP scalar; (c) a calibrated estimator with zero-injection bias below the signal threshold; (d) a V_{T}/V_{R} relation derived from the device scattering matrix. Until these are completed, graphene interferometry remains a candidate platform, not a decisive test.

### 5.10 Forensic Analysis: Identified Errors and Corrected Criteria

After classifying the result as pipeline-limited, we conducted a forensic analysis to identify the specific errors in the analysis chain. Nine targeted investigations (F1–F9) were executed, each addressing one link in the theory-to-observable chain. Three confirmed errors were identified, one estimator was repaired, and the corrected V_{T}/V_{R} criterion was verified.

**F1 — V_{T}/V_{R} transfer relation (CONFIRMED ERROR).** The previous analysis tested for opposite *phase* between V_{T} and V_{R} channels (δθ_{R} = −δθ_{T}). This is incorrect. A Fabry–P&eacute;rot cavity satisfies T(θ) = T_{0} + V_{T} cos(θ), R(θ) = R_{0} − V_{R} cos(θ), so a common phase perturbation θ → θ + δθ gives δV_{T} = −V_{T} sin(θ) · δθ and δV_{R} = +V_{R} sin(θ) · δθ. The phase perturbation is COMMON (δθ_{R} = δθ_{T}); the voltage residuals are OPPOSITE (δV_{R} ≈ −(V_{R}/V_{T}) · δV_{T}). Numerical verification on the Zimmermann data confirms: phase residual correlation = 0.981 (positively correlated = common phase), same-sign fraction = 84.6%. The previous 44–50% opposite-phase fraction was testing the wrong criterion. The corrected opposite-voltage criterion gives 100% consistency across all nine line cuts.

**F2 — Directional projection in Dirac phase (CONFIRMED ERROR).** The disformal phase correction is derived from the effective Dirac action on the disformal metric g̃_{μν} = A²(φ) g_{μν} + B(φ) ∇_{μ}φ ∇_{ν}φ. The eikonal equation gives δΘ_{B} = ∫ (B/(2A²)) (u·∇φ)² dℓ, where u is the edge-state propagation direction. This involves the DIRECTIONAL projection (u·∇φ)² = |∇φ|² cos²(α), where α is the angle between propagation and field gradient. The current predictor uses |∇φ|² (isotropic), which overestimates the signal by a factor of &sim;15.5×: edge states propagate perpendicular to the field gradient for most of their path (cos²(α) ≈ 0), with significant projection only at the constriction (cos²(α) ≈ 1). Only 3.2% of the edge path has significant directional projection.

**F3 — Real-space electrostatic model (CONFIRMED ERROR).** The current predictor uses gate-voltage derivatives (∂U/∂V_{sg}, ∂U/∂V_{bg}) as proxies for the real-space electric field. A 2D Poisson solver on the actual device cross-section (hBN dielectric, split gates, SiO_{2} substrate) shows that the real-space field profile differs dramatically from the gate-voltage proxy. The directional/isotropic ratio from the real-space model is &sim;0.0004 at representative voltages, meaning the current predictor overestimates the signal by up to &sim;2500×. The physical predictor must be computed from the real-space field with the directional projection from F2.

**F5 — Estimator repair (CONFIRMED ERROR, REPAIRED).** The nonlinear model y = A cos(kx + φ + κ · T_{geom}) + ... places κ inside the cosine, creating a degenerate ridge with the phase parameters φ and P. Zero-injection bias for the nonlinear model is 8.55 ± 1.23. Linearizing the model (valid for δθ << 1 rad, confirmed in Section 5.5) by moving κ outside the cosine: y = A cos(kx + φ) + ... + κ · T_{geom} + ε, reduces the zero-injection bias to −0.365 ± 2.891 — a 23× improvement. The profile likelihood for κ (optimizing nuisance at each fixed κ) gives MLE κ = 1.90 with 95% CI [−5, +5], confirming that the data do not constrain κ.

**F6 — Nuisance-model absorption (CONFIRMED LIMITATION).** Injection of known TEP-like signals into the data shows that the single-carrier nuisance model absorbs 86.7% of the injected signal, and the two-carrier model absorbs 119.5% (more than 100% — it amplifies the absorption). This means the residual-fitting architecture is fundamentally limited: even if a real TEP signal exists at the predicted amplitude, the nuisance model can absorb it. This is not a calibration issue but a structural identifiability problem.

**F7 — Joint fit with corrected V_{T}/V_{R} criterion.** Using the corrected criterion from F1 (common phase, opposite voltage), the joint model is: V_{T} = A_{T} cos(kx+φ) + B_{T}x + C_{T}x² + off_{T} + κ · T_{geom} + ε_{T}, V_{R} = A_{R} cos(kx+φ) + B_{R}x + C_{R}x² + off_{R} − λ·κ · T_{geom} + ε_{R}, where λ = V_{R}/V_{T} = 0.933. The joint κ = −0.261. Separate fits give κ_{T} = −0.319, κ_{R} = +0.191 — signs are opposite as predicted. The opposite-voltage fraction across nine line cuts is 100%, compared to 44–50% (chance) for the old opposite-phase criterion. This confirms that the corrected criterion is the right test, but consistency alone is not detection.

**F8 — Expected detectability.** The Fisher information for κ in the linearized model, after profiling over nuisance parameters, is I = 10.55. The minimum detectable κ at 95% confidence is 0.506; at 99% confidence, 0.716. The measured κ = −0.319 is below the 95% threshold. The sensitivity table confirms: 86% detection power at κ = 0.5, 98.5% at κ = 1.0, and 100% at κ ≥ 2.0. The non-detection is consistent with the sensitivity limit: the signal (if present) is below the calibrated detection threshold.

**F9 — Held-out validation.** Twenty random 60/40 train-test splits produce a mean held-out improvement of 0.00% (65% of splits show positive improvement). The training κ = −0.207 ± 0.155. The result is inconclusive: the TEP predictor does not clearly improve held-out prediction, consistent with a signal below the detection threshold.

**Forensic summary.** The forensic analysis confirms the pipeline-limited non-detection classification and identifies three specific errors in the previous pipeline: (1) the V_{T}/V_{R} criterion tested opposite phase instead of opposite voltage, (2) the predictor used |∇φ|² instead of (u·∇φ)², overestimating by 15.5×, (3) the predictor used gate-voltage derivatives instead of real-space fields, overestimating by up to 2500×. The estimator bias was repaired from 8.55 to 0.365 by linearization. The corrected V_{T}/V_{R} criterion gives 100% opposite-voltage consistency (vs 44% chance for the wrong criterion), but the measured κ (−0.32) is below the 95% detectability threshold (0.506), and nuisance models absorb 87–120% of injected signals. The path to a decisive test requires: (a) a normalized mesoscopic screening law, (b) a real-space electrostatic model with directional projection, (c) a joint model that does not allow nuisance absorption, (d) a calibrated estimator with confirmed zero-injection bias.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper develops the proposal that virtual force carriers and statistical wavefunctions are tangent-limit constructs arising in candidate low-screening measurement channels where the local screening projection is weak and boundary conditions permit observable disformal response, so that the assumption of a flat, isochronous background holds only approximately. In that sector, interactions are modeled through the disformal coupling B(φ), and measurement is treated as the geometric sampling of shared temporal shear contours. As the screening projection strengthens, the observable disformal response is continuously suppressed and standard perturbation theory is recovered as the tangent limit. The primary theoretical result is the derivation of the disformal interferometric phase from the particle action: δθ(x) = κ · T_{geom}(x), where T_{geom} is the line integral of B[φ(r)]|∇φ(r)|^{2} along the interferometer arm. This phase is additive, separates cleanly from dynamical and Aharonov-Bohm terms, and is not reducible to any combination of carrier period, linear drift, or sinusoidal nuisance basis.

The empirical analysis proceeds in two stages. First, we establish an identifiability obstruction: a global temporal rescaling γ is exactly degenerate with carrier period (P_{eff} = P/γ) on a single line cut, and a generic affine predictor along the gate-voltage cut is collinear with conventional phase drift (VIF → ∞). These are structural results, not pipeline artefacts. Second, we execute a candidate decisive analysis on the Zimmermann et al. (2017) dataset: a frozen geometry-locked predictor T_{geom} from an analytical electrostatic model of the split-gate device, a joint generative model y = M_{conv} + κ · R_{c} · T_{geom} + ε with AR(1) correlated likelihood, seven conventional baseline models, five phase extraction methods with injection-recovery validation, full-map V_{T}/V_{R} replication across nine line cuts, covariance stress testing (white, AR(1), ARMA(1,1)), and held-out validation.

Zero of six prespecified detection criteria are met. However, this outcome is not a quantitative null. A forensic analysis (F1–F9) identifies three specific errors in the pipeline and repairs one. First, the V_{T}/V_{R} criterion tested opposite phase instead of opposite voltage: the Fabry–P&eacute;rot scattering matrix predicts common phase with opposite voltage residuals, confirmed by phase correlation 0.981 and 100% opposite-voltage consistency across nine cuts (vs 44% for the wrong criterion). Second, the predictor uses |∇φ|² (isotropic) instead of (u·∇φ)² = |∇φ|² cos²(α) (directional), overestimating by 15.5× because edge states propagate perpendicular to the field gradient for most of their path. Third, the predictor uses gate-voltage derivatives instead of real-space fields from a Poisson solver, overestimating by up to 2500×. The estimator bias is repaired from 8.55 to 0.365 by linearization. The measured κ = −0.32 is below the 95% detectability threshold (0.506, Fisher information I = 10.55). Nuisance models absorb 87–120% of injected signals. TEP does not presently provide a normalized device-scale screening law, so the question of whether this device should have detected TEP has no calculable answer.

The correct classification is a pipeline-limited non-detection of a candidate mesoscopic proxy. The non-detection constrains the tested proxy and analysis architecture, but not the underlying disformal coupling. Graphene quantum Hall interferometry is a plausible candidate platform for mesoscopic TEP tests because it combines coherent phase accumulation with tunable boundary geometry. However, TEP does not presently provide a normalized device-scale screening law or a first-principles mapping from gate electrostatics to the temporal scalar field. The Zimmermann dataset therefore cannot yet be classified as an experiment in which a detectable TEP response was predicted. The major result of this paper is the identification of what must be calculated before the question becomes meaningful: (a) a normalized mesoscopic screening law with fixed amplitude prediction; (b) a real-space electrostatic model mapping device geometry to the TEP scalar; (c) a calibrated estimator with zero-injection bias below the signal threshold; (d) a V_{T}/V_{R} relation derived from the device scattering matrix.

The theoretical constructions developed in this paper include: the homogeneous Maxwell equation (Bianchi identity) satisfied by the U(1) phase bundle curvature (Proposition 1), the Aharonov-Bohm holonomy structure from flux quantisation (Proposition 2), Coulomb's law recovered as a conditional weak-field theorem (Proposition 3), the Bell correlation recovered as a conditional SU(2) holonomy construction (Proposition 4), and a candidate detector-flux identification to the Born rule in the screened tangent limit (Proposition 5). The disformal interferometric phase derivation (Section 3.6) provides the observable that connects theory to experiment. The inhomogeneous Maxwell equation, photon propagating mode equation, and full Hilbert-space Born rule are not derived here; they are reserved for a forthcoming companion paper. The SU(2) weak-interaction sector is constructed as a candidate Yang-Mills embedding on the disformal metric (Section 7.1), not as an emergence of weak interactions from scalar-disformal geometry. The quantum foundations of this framework — including the derivation of the Klein-Gordon and Dirac operators from dynamical proper-time geometry, and the geometric reinterpretation of spin and antiparticle structure — are established in the companion paper TEP-QF (Paper 23, Qatar).

This analysis is based on the Zimmermann et al. (2017) published graphene device, which remains the only publicly deposited raw Fabry-Perot interferometry dataset with full measurement files (Zenodo 4430703). The joint generative model and geometry-locked predictor framework developed here are general and apply to any quantum Hall Fabry-Perot or Mach-Zehnder geometry. The immediate priorities are specific: (1) completing the mesoscopic screening normalization so that TEP can predict a phase amplitude for a given device; (2) building a real-space electrostatic model from actual device layouts rather than analytical proxies; (3) deriving the V_{T}/V_{R} channel relation from the device scattering matrix; (4) repairing the estimator so that zero-injection bias falls below the signal threshold; (5) devices with deliberately asymmetric arm configurations that maximize the differential temporal geometry sampled by the two interfering paths; (6) a Mach-Zehnder geometry with unequal arm lengths that breaks the P–γ degeneracy structurally. ## 7. Theoretical Horizons and Future Work

The empirical programme of this paper rests on the candidate U(1) phase-bundle construction, mesoscopic boundary geometry, and the disformal coupling B(φ) in candidate low-screening measurement channels where the temporal-field gradient |∂φ|^{2} is strong enough to produce observable disformal response. Several theoretical extensions are natural but are deliberately deferred here so that Section 2 can remain focused on the phase-bundle dynamics that directly motivate the graphene interferometry analysis.

### 7.1 A Generic Non-Abelian Yang-Mills Embedding

The SU(2) weak-interaction sector is addressed by extending the U(1) temporal phase bundle to a non-Abelian SU(2) phase bundle. The temporal orientation bundle of TEP-SPIN (Theorem 1, Paper 24) already carries an SU(2) double-cover structure for spinorial holonomy. The weak-interaction SU(2) is a distinct but parallel construction: it acts on an internal isospin doublet rather than the spinorial temporal orientation. **This section constructs a generic non-Abelian Yang-Mills embedding on the disformal metric; it is not yet a weak-interaction construction.** The observed weak interaction is chiral and couples SU(2) to left-handed doublets via the projector P_{L} = (1 − γ^{5})/2. The displayed fermion coupling below is vectorlike rather than chiral. A credible weak-sector embedding eventually needs chiral projectors, hypercharge assignments Y, anomaly cancellation, Yukawa couplings, and generation structure. Furthermore, an isolated complex SU(2) doublet does not produce the physical electroweak residual U(1)_{em}: with an SU(2) doublet alone, the gauge group is generically fully broken; an adjoint triplet can instead leave a U(1) subgroup. The physical W± and Z^{0} pattern requires the full SU(2)_{L} × U(1)_{Y} gauge structure with hypercharge and Weinberg mixing.

**Construction.** Let χ^{a} (a = 1, 2, 3) be a triplet of compact temporal phase fields taking values in the SU(2) Lie algebra. The gauge connection is the su(2)-valued one-form W_{μ} = W_{μ}^{a} τ^{a}/2, where τ^{a} are the Pauli matrices. The covariant derivative on an isospin doublet Ψ is:

D_{μ}Ψ = (∂_{μ} + igW_{μ}^{a}τ^{a}/2) Ψ, (15)

and the field strength is the curvature of the SU(2) bundle:

G^{a}_{μν} = ∂_{[μ}W^{a}_{ν]} − gε^{abc}W^{b}_{μ}W^{c}_{ν} = (1/ig)[D_{μ}, D_{ν}]^{a}, (16)

which transforms covariantly under the adjoint representation by construction. The SU(2) bundle action on the disformal metric is the natural non-Abelian extension of the U(1) phase bundle construction (Eqs. (2)–(3)):

S_{SU(2)} = ∫ d^{4}x √−g̃ [ −¼ g̃^{μα}g̃^{νβ} G^{a}_{μν}G^{a}_{αβ} + Ψ̄ iγ^{a} e_{a}^{μ} (∂_{μ} + Ω_{μ} + igW_{μ}^{b}τ^{b}/2) Ψ ], (17)

where Ψ is the isospin doublet fermion field, e_{a}^{μ} is the tetrad of the disformal metric g̃_{μν}, and Ω_{μ} is the spin connection. The tetrad satisfies g̃_{μν} = e_{μ}^{a}e_{ν}^{b}η_{ab}. Constructing e_{a}^{μ} and Ω_{μ} explicitly for the disformal metric g̃_{μν} = A^{2}g_{μν} + B ∇_{μ}φ ∇_{ν}φ is deferred to the companion paper. Varying with respect to W^{a}_{ν} yields the Yang-Mills equation on the disformal metric:

∇_{μ}^{(g̃)}G^{a}^{μν} + gε^{abc}W^{b}_{μ}G^{c}^{μν} = J^{a}^{ν}, (18)

where J^{a}^{ν} = gΨ̄γ^{ν}τ^{a}Ψ/2 is the isospin current. In the screened tangent limit (g̃_{μν} → η_{μν}), this reduces to the standard Yang-Mills equation. The Bianchi identity D_{[μ}G^{a}_{νρ]} = 0 follows from the Jacobi identity [D_{μ}, [D_{ν}, D_{ρ}]] + cyclic = 0, which is the non-Abelian generalization of dF = 0.

**Spontaneous symmetry breaking.** The Higgs mechanism is implemented by introducing a temporal phase doublet Φ with potential V(Φ) = λ(Φ̄Φ − v^{2}/2)^{2}. The vacuum expectation value &lang;Φ&rang; = (0, v/√2) breaks the SU(2) isospin symmetry. In the TEP framework, the Higgs field Φ is interpreted as a temporal phase condensate — a coherent state of the compact temporal phase fields χ^{a} that acquires a vacuum expectation value through the disformal coupling. The symmetry-breaking scale v ≈ 246 GeV is a parameter of the temporal phase potential, analogous to the scalar potential V(φ) that determines the conformal factor mass in TEP-SPIN (Paper 24, §2.3). The physical electroweak pattern — massive W± and Z^{0} bosons with a massless photon — requires the full SU(2)_{L} × U(1)_{Y} gauge structure with hypercharge and Weinberg mixing, not the isolated SU(2) → U(1) breaking shown here. The construction below provides the candidate SU(2) sector; the U(1)_{Y} hypercharge extension and Weinberg angle remain to be incorporated.

**Status.** The SU(2) sector is a candidate generic Yang-Mills embedding on the disformal metric, not a weak-interaction construction. The covariant derivative (15) and curvature (16) are mathematically consistent and reduce to standard Yang-Mills theory in the screened tangent limit. However, several technical issues remain: the curved-space fermion term in the action (17) requires tetrads and a spin connection that are not yet explicitly constructed; the displayed fermion action has unresolved spacetime/tangent-space indices; the fermion coupling is vectorlike rather than chiral — the observed weak interaction couples only to left-handed doublets via P_{L} = (1 − γ^{5})/2; an isolated SU(2) doublet does not leave a residual U(1)_{em} — the full SU(2)_{L} × U(1)_{Y} structure with hypercharge and Weinberg mixing is needed to obtain the physical photon and Z^{0}; and quantitative matching to measured W and Z masses, CKM matrix elements, and weak-interaction cross-sections requires specifying the temporal phase potential V(Φ) and the disformal coupling at the electroweak scale. The sector is therefore a candidate geometric construction with the correct mathematical structure, pending both technical completion and phenomenological calibration.

This construction must be distinguished from the SU(2) holonomy of the temporal orientation bundle invoked in Section 2.5. That group governs spin-1/2 parallel transport along entanglement contours and is part of the established TEP-SPIN programme (Paper 24, Theorem 1). The weak-interaction SU(2) is a parallel internal symmetry construction on the same disformal manifold; both use SU(2) connections but act on different fibre spaces (temporal orientation vs. isospin).

### 7.3 Foundational Status

The theoretical constructions completed in this paper are: the homogeneous Maxwell equation (Bianchi identity) from the U(1) phase bundle curvature (Proposition 1), the Aharonov-Bohm holonomy structure from flux quantisation (Proposition 2), Coulomb's law as a conditional weak-field theorem (Proposition 3), the Bell correlation as a conditional SU(2) holonomy construction (Proposition 4), and a candidate detector-flux identification to the |Ψ|^{2} rule in the screened tangent limit (Proposition 5). The inhomogeneous Maxwell equation, the photon propagating mode equation, coupling to matter currents, and the full Hilbert-space Born rule are not derived here; they are reserved for a forthcoming companion paper. The SU(2) weak-interaction sector is constructed as a candidate Yang-Mills embedding on the disformal metric (Section 7.1), not as an emergence of weak interactions from scalar-disformal geometry. The Bell correlation derivation (Proposition 4) assumes a spinorial SU(2) structure and singlet-like anti-alignment; it is a conditional reconstruction using quantum ingredients, not yet a derivation from scalar-disformal geometry alone. The deterministic contour model does not, by itself, evade Bell's locality assumptions; a finite-speed pre-existing contour is compatible with Bell's theorem as a non-local hidden-variable model. Remaining foundational tasks include: (i) the inhomogeneous Maxwell equation and photon mode from a matter action on the disformal metric, (ii) the full Born rule from Hilbert-space structure, (iii) quantitative electroweak phenomenology (W/Z masses, CKM matrix) from the temporal phase potential, and (iv) metric completion for non-Abelian internal sectors that may require additional orientation variables or multiplet structure beyond the minimal disformal ansatz.
